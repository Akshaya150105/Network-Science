# Limitations (statistics side)

*The data-side limitations (prompts, API behaviour, parsing, cost) are written by Akshaya.*

* **Small n compared with Paper B.** Paper B's asymptotic results and tables use 50,000 to 100,000 edges; our LLM runs have 500 and our classical runs 500 to 2,000. We therefore calibrate the critical values by Monte Carlo at the run length instead of relying on the Brownian-bridge limit, and we cannot reproduce Paper B's numbers, only their trends. At n = 500 the power is low (28% for a shift from delta = 1 to 3), so a non-detection is weak evidence of no change.
* **A single LLM.** All LLM results come from one model (the model swap is optional). Paper A reports clear differences between models, so the results may not carry over to other models or providers.
* **Few replicates.** The LLM experiments have about ten runs per condition, because every run costs thousands of API calls. Rejection rates and delta estimates therefore have wide standard errors, and sign and Wilcoxon tests on ten runs have little power. The classical benchmarks use 100 runs per cell (Type I error: 500 fresh networks).
* **The test assumes exact PA(delta).** It is a likelihood-ratio test of a *specific* model, with one affine attachment rule and one edge per new node. Any departure (hubs that persist regardless of degree, dependence on the candidate order, the random fallbacks after a parse failure) is a model misspecification that the test can register as a changepoint or hide as noise. The no-shock control is the only check of this, and it is itself a small sample.
* **Calibration at one null value.** Critical values were calibrated with delta = 1. The statistic is asymptotically free of delta, and a check at delta = -0.5, 0, 1 and 2 (Controls, section 2) gave Type I errors between 0.03 and 0.064. Values of delta far outside that range were not checked.
* **Estimation at the estimated changepoint.** delta1_hat and delta2_hat at tau_hat are selection-biased; we also report them at the known tau, which is only possible because we control when the intervention happens.
* **The changepoint is not always identifiable.** Picks of a degree-2 node carry no information about delta, so the maximiser of the statistic can be non-unique; we resolve ties by taking the leftmost split within 1e-9 of the maximum.
* **The non-parametric competitor is limited by design.** With the paper's default h_n = log log n it cannot detect a change before 0.55 n at n = 500, so it is compared only at n = 2000, and with a single choice of tuning sequences.
* **Single changepoint.** We test for exactly one changepoint with trim 0.1; changes in the first or last 10% of a run cannot be detected, and multiple changepoints (Paper B, Section V [arXiv: Section 4]) are not used.
* **Misspecification can look like a changepoint.** The published version of Paper B (Section IV-D, after Table VI; this passage is not in arXiv v3) says it expects the likelihood method to falter under uniform attachment: the estimated delta can drift as the network grows, producing artificial, changepoint-like behaviour. LLM-grown networks need not be exactly PA(delta), so a rejection is only evidence of a *shift in the fitted delta*, and the no-shock control runs are the check on how often that happens.
* **Shift size.** Paper B notes that typical delta in real networks lies in (-1, 1) (Section III-A). In our n = 500 benchmark the largest shift in that width (a change of 2 units, delta 1 -> 3) is detected 28% of the time, and smaller shifts at most 15% of the time (delta1 = 1; we did not compute power for shifts inside (-1, 1) itself). A moderate LLM effect could therefore go undetected at this run length.
